<Lead>
On 14 September 1989, police in Colorado Springs opened a rented storage locker and found thirty pounds of explosives, three pipe bombs, ten handguns with the serial numbers filed off, and a set of surveillance packets naming targets in Denver, Los Angeles and Tucson. The Tucson packet contained photographs of Masjid Tucson from every angle, interior shots included, notes on the neighbourhood, and a section headed "further info for final setup." The material dated from around mid-1984. Four and a half months later, Dr. Rashad Khalifa was murdered in that mosque's kitchen.
</Lead>

This editorial sets out what the primary record establishes: the declassified FBI teletypes released under FOIA, the findings of the Pima County Medical Examiner, the trial court's ruling of December 2012, and the memorandum decision of the Arizona Court of Appeals of March 2014. Where the record is silent or divided, that is stated rather than filled in.

---

## 1. The Plan, Mid-1984

The surveillance packet was not aimed at a building. It was aimed at a man.

FBI Phoenix summarised the Tucson packet for receiving offices in a teletype of early February 1990.<Ref id="1" /> The packet was "very detailed" and included "a stack of 35 millimeter color photographs of the Mosque from all angles, as well as interior shots," together with "details about the neighborhood, best methods to be used to kill the individual, and the best time to do it." Under the heading "further info for final setup," the plan specified that a participant would attend the mosque a week in advance to learn the crowd size and the subject's movements.

The teletype then quotes the packet's contingency section verbatim. It is the coldest sentence in the archive:

<PullQuote attribution="Surveillance packet recovered at Colorado Springs, quoted in FBI Phoenix teletype, February 1990">
He may not be there. Proposed solution is as we wait, everyone who comes in must be eliminated until he shows up. If for some reason he doesn't, then that's all we can do.
</PullQuote>

The FBI's own assessment of the packet was blunt: "it appears that KHALIFA was the probable target for this attack," and "circumstances surrounding the murder of KHALIFA are very similar to the FUQRA target information packet found for Tucson."

The locker also held, alongside the explosives and the weapons, "extensive writings linking the group to a 'Holy war in the United States against perceived religious and ethnic enemies.'"<Ref id="1" />

## 2. 31 January 1990

The mosque's secretary arrived before sunrise and found the body on the kitchen floor.<Ref id="2" />

The medical examiner, Dr. Bruce Parks, documented at least twenty-nine sharp force wounds. The appellate decision records twenty-nine sharp force and eighteen blunt force wounds, some inflicted with enough force to break bone, to the head, neck, torso and back. Death occurred at around one in the morning. The assault took place after the mosque had been locked for the night, and there was no evidence that anyone other than the victim and his assailant was inside.<Ref id="3" />

The killer then attempted to destroy the scene. A chemical solvent had been poured over the kitchen floor and over the body, and the gas burners on the stove had been turned on. The fire never started: the pilot light did not ignite. The FBI teletypes name the solvent as Xylol and record one further detail that no accident explains, and that the archive holds because it settles the question of intent on its own: **the retail price tag had been scraped off the can.**<Ref id="4" />

<Verse chapter={2} verses="87">
We gave Moses the scripture, and subsequent to him we sent other messengers, and we gave Jesus, son of Mary, profound miracles and supported him with the Holy Spirit. Is it not a fact that every time a messenger went to you with anything you disliked, your ego caused you to be arrogant? Some of them you rejected, and some of them you killed.
</Verse>

## 3. Twenty-Two Years

A man calling himself Ben Phillips had begun attending the mosque three to four weeks before the murder, presenting himself as someone who knew nothing about the religion and wished to learn. He walked the private areas during social gatherings: the kitchen, and Khalifa's office. He left Tucson immediately afterwards.<Ref id="3" />

The trail went cold. In 1994 an FBI agent located the same man in Dallas, living under a second alias, Joseph Wall, and took fingerprints and hair samples. Nothing was done with them for twelve years. In 2006 the Tucson Police Department stood up a cold-case unit and sent the original crime-scene samples for re-analysis. One did not match the victim. It matched Glen Cusford Francis. Re-examining the jacket Khalifa had been wearing, investigators found a second, previously unanalysed bloodstain. It matched Francis too.

He was charged in 2009 and tried in 2012. His defence was misidentification, and its argument to the jury was that the science did not place him at the scene and that, in any case, "everybody in the world wanted to kill" the victim.<Ref id="3" />

The motive was put before the jury by a former FBI agent, who testified to what Francis had said to him about the victim:

<PullQuote attribution="Former FBI agent's trial testimony, recorded in State v. Francis, Arizona Court of Appeals, 2014">
if [R.K.] proved parts of the Koran wrong, then as a Muslim, he deserved what he got.
</PullQuote>

The judge refused to instruct the jury on second-degree murder or manslaughter, finding the record devoid of "evidence whatsoever . . . to sustain a finding that this homicide was anything other than premeditated." The jury returned a verdict of guilty of first-degree murder on **19 December 2012**. Francis was sentenced to life with the possibility of release after twenty-five years, the only sentence available under the law as it stood in 1990. The Arizona Court of Appeals affirmed the conviction on **19 March 2014**.<Ref id="3" />

## 4. What the Numbers Do and Do Not Show

The archive publishes the following observations with their arithmetic exposed, and with the same caution it applies everywhere else: a serial number or a headcount that an institution fixed for its own reasons is a different class of evidence from an interval measured between two dates a tabulator chose.

**The date of birth.** Khalifa was born on **19 November 1935**, and he says so himself. In a Friday sermon delivered on 26 November 1989, one week after his fifty-fourth birthday and sixty-six days before he was killed, he thanked the congregation for celebrating it with him and then worked through a calendar of day-numbers:<Ref id="5" />

<PullQuote attribution="Dr. Rashad Khalifa, Friday Sermon, 26 November 1989">
November 19. That was the Birthday and I was thanking you for celebrating it with me. And this is a calendar that gives the number of every day of the year. It just happens that November 19 is the only day of the year whose number is a multiple of 19 [...] Now, January 19 is the 19th day of the year. So, you don't count that. [...] November 19th is number 323. It's a multiple of 19.
</PullQuote>

The arithmetic checks: 19 November is the 323rd day of a common year, and 323 = 19 × 17. He excludes 19 January himself, and gives his reason, so the claim is qualified rather than overstated.

The FBI file gives 18 November 1935 in two places, once in an Immigration and Naturalization Service file note and once in an agent's write-up of an interview on 13 September 1985.<Ref id="6" /> The archive treats that as an administrative error rather than a rival account. A federal case file reconstructing the birth date of a man born in Kafr Zayat in 1935 is working from immigration paperwork at several removes from the event; the man himself, naming the day from the pulpit while his congregation is still marking it, is not.

**The span of the life.** From 19 November 1935 to 31 January 1990, counting both endpoints, is 19,798 days, and 19,798 = **19 × 1042**.

**The verdict and the affirmance.** The jury convicted on the nineteenth of the month; the Court of Appeals affirmed on the nineteenth of the month. These are observations about a calendar, not multiples of nineteen, and any given date has roughly a one-in-thirty chance of falling on a nineteenth. They are recorded because they are checkable, not because they prove anything.

**What was tested.** Eight anchor dates were run against each other under both counting conventions, fifty-four trials in all, and four produced multiples of nineteen where chance predicts about three. One of the four was discarded because it depended on dating the 1974 discovery to a specific day the archive does not have. A reader who assembles a different set of anchors should expect a comparable yield from any chronology whatever.

The claim that carries weight here is not on this list. It is the one Khalifa spent sixteen years documenting, and it does not depend on any date at all: the parameters of the Quran were fixed before anyone counted them.

## 5. What the Killing Did Not Accomplish

Two attempts were made that night to destroy a record, and both failed.

The first was the solvent and the gas. Had the pilot light caught, the kitchen, the body and the forensic evidence would have burned together, and the DNA that convicted a man twenty-two years later would not have survived to be re-analysed in 2006.

The second was the killing itself. By January 1990 the authorised English translation and its appendices were finished. The mathematical work was published, in print, in circulation, and reproducible by anyone with a copy of the Quran and a calculator. It required no living author to defend it and no institution to authenticate it. What was on paper stayed on paper.

<PullQuote attribution="PROVENANCE RULE">
Evidence that anyone can recount for themselves cannot be assassinated. The archive's task is only to keep the sources reachable so that the recount stays possible.
</PullQuote>

The bulletin he edited kept publishing. The January 1990 issue, number 61, was the first to carry the masthead *Submitters Perspective*; three more issues followed him into the record. The primary materials, the newsletters, the audio and video recordings, the correspondence and the case file, remain available to be checked against each other, including the parts that disagree.
